Your Website Is Talking to Machines, Too

Share

Picture the owner of a plumbing and heating company who decides, out of curiosity, to ask an AI assistant about his own business. The answer comes back confident and polished. It also lists opening hours he changed last spring, mentions a water-heater offer that ended two years ago, and says the company serves a town it stopped covering when a technician retired. None of it is malicious. The assistant simply pieced together what it could find, and some of what it found was out of date or unclear.

That small moment captures a big shift. For most of the internet's history, websites were built for one audience: people sitting in front of a browser. That is no longer the whole picture. Search crawlers, AI systems and a growing list of automated tools now visit your site constantly, and what they take away shapes how your business is represented in places you never see directly.

These machine visitors do not admire your photography or notice your carefully chosen brand colors. They read the structure, the text and the code underneath. If that layer is clear, accurate and consistent, they tend to describe you well. If it is messy, thin or contradictory, they fill the gaps with guesses, outdated pages or information from somewhere else entirely.

In this article, we look at who these non-human visitors are, how they actually read a page, what they need from you, and why keeping them well informed is a business decision rather than a technical footnote. You will also get a simple first step you can take this week, with no code required.

Who is visiting besides people

Some of your machine visitors are old acquaintances. Search engine crawlers have been visiting websites for decades, following links from page to page and building the index that powers ordinary search results. If your business shows up when someone searches for a service in your area, a crawler read your site first.

Others are newer. AI companies run their own crawlers that read public web pages, and AI assistants may fetch pages while answering a question so they can summarize or cite them. When a customer asks an assistant to recommend a family dentist nearby or explain what a particular accounting firm specializes in, the answer is built from text that a machine gathered and interpreted.

Then there is a long tail of quieter visitors. Social platforms and messaging apps fetch your page to build a preview card when someone shares a link. Directories and review platforms pull details about your business. Screen readers interpret your pages for people who cannot see them, and they rely on many of the same structural signals that crawlers do.

The common thread is that none of these visitors experiences your website the way a person with a fast laptop does. They work from what is written and how it is organized. That makes the underlying quality of your pages more important than ever.

How a machine actually reads your page

When a person lands on your homepage, they take in the layout at a glance. A large photo, a bold headline and a bright button tell them what matters. A machine gets none of that visual hierarchy. It reads the page as a document: headings, paragraphs, links, lists, image descriptions and a handful of behind-the-scenes labels such as the page title and meta description.

This is where many attractive websites fall short. If your services are listed inside a graphic, a machine may see nothing but a file name. If your opening hours live only in a photo of your front door, they may as well not exist. If your main headline is a slogan such as "Built on Excellence," a machine learns nothing about what you sell or where you sell it.

Content that loads only after scripts run, or that sits inside interactive tabs, sliders and pop-ups, can also be harder to read. Some automated visitors do not process pages the way a full browser does, so information that appears only after a click or a delay may be missed. The same goes for key details tucked away in downloadable PDFs rather than written on the page itself.

A useful test is to imagine your page printed as plain text with every image, color and animation stripped away. Would a stranger reading that printout understand who you are, what you offer, who you serve and how to reach you? If the answer is yes, machines will usually understand it too. If the answer is "sort of," that gap is where misunderstandings begin.

What these visitors need from you

The good news is that machine-friendly websites are rarely exotic. They are simply well-built websites with a little extra care in a few places. When we review sites at Codelynx Digital, the same handful of fundamentals comes up again and again:

  • Clear structure: one main heading per page, logical subheadings beneath it, and pages organized so each one has a single, obvious purpose.
  • Readable text: important information written as real text, with descriptive alternative text on meaningful images.
  • Fast, reliable pages: slow, broken or error-prone pages may be skipped, crawled less often or represented poorly.
  • Consistent facts: the same name, address, phone number, hours and services as your listings elsewhere on the web.
  • Structured data: behind-the-scenes labels that state key facts, such as your business type, location and contact details, in a format machines can read directly.

None of these is a trick, and each one also helps human visitors. Clear headings help a busy customer scan a page. Fast pages keep people from giving up. Consistent facts stop someone from driving to an old address.

Structured data deserves a brief note because it is the item most business owners have never heard of. It is a standardized way of labeling information so a machine does not have to infer it from the surrounding text. We cover it in more depth in our guide to structuring your data for AI-powered search, but the short version is that it removes ambiguity where ambiguity is costly.

Consistent facts, everywhere they appear

Machines rarely rely on a single source. A search engine or AI assistant may compare what your website says with your business profile, directory listings, social media pages, review sites and news mentions. When those sources agree, the system can state facts about you with confidence. When they disagree, it has to choose, and it will not always choose correctly.

Consider a law firm that moved offices a few years ago. The website shows the new address, but an old directory listing still has the previous one, and an announcement about the move mentions both. A person would figure it out. A machine might blend the details, hedge its answer or send a prospective client to an empty building. The same thing happens with phone numbers, opening hours, service areas and business names that changed slightly over time.

The fix is unglamorous but effective. Decide on a single source of truth for your core business facts, usually your own website, and make sure every other listing matches it exactly. Then build a habit of updating everything at once whenever something changes. Many businesses update their website and forget the rest, or update their business profile and forget the website.

Your site also needs to agree with itself. It is surprisingly common to find a footer with one phone number, a contact page with another and an old landing page with a third. Each inconsistency gives a machine a reason to doubt the others. Deciding who gets access

Your website can tell automated visitors which areas they are welcome to read. The most common tool for this is a small file called robots.txt, which sits at the root of your site and gives instructions to crawlers. Well-behaved crawlers, including those run by major search engines and AI companies, generally respect it, and some AI providers publish the names of their crawlers so site owners can allow or block them individually.

That turns a technical setting into a business choice. Some organizations, such as publishers whose content is their product, may decide to limit how AI systems use their pages. Most service businesses, retailers and manufacturers want the opposite: they want assistants to understand them well enough to recommend them. Neither choice is wrong, but it should be a deliberate decision rather than an accident.

Accidents are more common than you might think. We regularly see sites that were blocked from all crawlers during development and never unblocked at launch, sites where an overly broad rule hides the very pages that matter most, and test copies of websites left open to crawlers where they compete with the real thing. Any of these can quietly undermine years of work.

You may also hear about newer conventions, such as a plain-text summary file sometimes called llms.txt that points AI tools toward a site's most important pages. These ideas are still evolving and support varies, so treat them as a helpful extra rather than a replacement for the fundamentals. A practical first step you can take this week

When machines misunderstand your website, the consequences are real. You might be left out of recommendations, described inaccurately or confused with a competitor that has a similar name. Because this happens out of sight, many businesses only discover a problem when a customer mentions something odd. Treating machine readability as part of your digital strategy protects your reputation in the places you cannot watch directly.

You do not need to start with a large project. Pick your five most important pages, typically the homepage, your main service pages and your contact page. Ask your web team to show you how each one looks with styling and images removed, or simply select all the text on the page and paste it into a plain document. Read it as if you knew nothing about your business.

Then ask three questions. Is it obvious what you do and who you do it for? Are your name, location, phone number and hours written as text and identical everywhere? Does each page have a clear main heading that describes its actual topic? Wherever the answer is no, you have found your first fix.

Your website now has two audiences. Design for people, and make sure the machines can follow along.

Finally, ask a couple of AI assistants a simple question about your business and note what they get right and wrong. The errors usually point straight back to an unclear page or an outdated listing. Ready to make your website easier for machines to understand?

Making your site readable for crawlers and AI assistants is not about chasing a trend. It is about making sure the most important facts about your business are clear, consistent and easy to find for every kind of visitor. The same work usually makes the site more useful for your customers, too.
