Planning a Multi-Location Website That Scales

Share

Picture a family dental practice that started with one clinic and now runs four. The website was built back when there was a single address. Since then, someone has added a Locations page with four addresses stacked on top of each other, a second phone number has crept into the footer, and the opening hours on the homepage only apply to the original clinic. A new patient searches for a dentist near the newest branch, calls the first number they see and reaches a receptionist across town who has to transfer them, if they stay on the line at all.

At Codelynx Digital, this is one of the most common problems we see when a business grows. The company has expanded carefully, but the website has expanded by accident. Every branch was squeezed into a structure designed for one location, and now each small update means hunting through pages to find every place the old information still lives.

It does not have to be this way. A multi-location website does not need to be complicated, but it does need a clear plan for what lives where. The same principles apply whether you run three dental clinics, a regional chain of hardware stores or a home-services company covering several cities.

In this guide, you will learn how to organize your site so it can grow, what every location page should include, how to decide which content is shared and which is local, how to make finding the right branch effortless, and how to turn adding a new location into a routine task instead of a redesign.

Start with a structure that expects more locations

The most important decisions happen before anyone writes a word of copy. Your site structure determines how easy it will be to add, update and remove branches for years to come.

For most businesses, the cleanest approach is a single website with one central locations hub and a dedicated page for each branch underneath it. A simple pattern such as /locations/ for the hub and /locations/riverside/ or /locations/downtown/ for each branch is easy for visitors to understand and easy for your team to repeat. Keeping everything on one domain also means every branch benefits from the reputation the whole brand has built, rather than splitting it across separate sites that each have to earn trust on their own.

Think of your services and your locations as two separate dimensions. Your services describe what you do, and your locations describe where you do it. Each service gets one strong page, each location gets one strong page, and the two link to each other. Resist the temptation to generate a page for every combination of service and city unless you genuinely have something different to say about each one. A handful of useful pages will serve you far better than hundreds of near-identical ones.

Finally, make locations easy to reach from anywhere. A Locations link in the main navigation and a short branch list in the footer give visitors a path to the right branch from any page.

Give every location a genuine page

Each branch deserves a page that could stand on its own. When someone lands on it from a search or a map listing, they should find everything they need to visit, call or book without going anywhere else. That usefulness is also what tells search engines the page is worth showing to people nearby.

A strong location page usually includes:

  • The essentials: full address, phone number, email and opening hours, including holiday hours.
  • Getting there: a map, directions, parking details and nearby landmarks.
  • What is offered here: the services available at this branch, especially where they differ from other locations.
  • The people: the manager, key staff or practitioners who work at this location.
  • Local proof: reviews from customers of this branch and photos of the actual building and team.

The trap to avoid is the copy-and-paste location page, where every branch has the same paragraph with only the city name swapped out. Those pages offer visitors nothing new, and search engines tend to treat them as thin or duplicate content. Instead, write a short introduction for each branch that reflects what is genuinely different about it. Maybe one clinic offers evening appointments, one store has a larger garden section, or one office is the only one with step-free access. Those details are exactly what a local customer wants to know.

Real photos matter more than most businesses expect. A picture of the storefront helps someone recognize it from the street, and a photo of the front desk team makes a first visit feel less like a gamble.

Decide what to share and what to keep local

Not everything needs to be written separately for each branch. In fact, much of your content should not be. Your brand story, core service descriptions, policies, warranties and general FAQs are the same no matter which location a customer visits, so they should live in one place and be referenced from every location page.

This matters for two reasons. First, it saves your team time, because an update to your returns policy or a service description happens once instead of four or ten times. Second, it keeps your information consistent. When the same service is described separately on every branch page, those descriptions slowly drift apart as different people edit them. Before long, one page promises a free consultation and another mentions a fee, and customers notice.

Location-specific content is everything that genuinely varies: the address, hours, phone number, team, reviews, local events and any exceptions to the norm. If one branch offers a service the others do not, or closes early on Fridays, that belongs on its own page.

Consider a law firm with three offices. Its practice area pages, explaining family law, property disputes or business contracts, are shared across the whole firm. Each office page lists the attorneys based there, links to the practice areas they handle and shows directions and parking details. A visitor can move naturally from "what does this firm do?" to "who can help me nearby?", and the firm maintains only one version of each practice description.

Make finding the right branch effortless

When someone arrives on your site, one of their first questions is often "which location is closest to me?" Answer it in seconds. A location finder, whether an interactive map, a searchable list or both, should let visitors search by city, neighborhood or postal code and see the nearest branches with their hours and phone numbers right away.

Do not rely on an embedded map alone. Maps can be slow on mobile, and the information inside them is not always easy for screen readers or search engines to read. Always include a plain text list of your branches alongside the map, with each one linking to its location page.

Once a visitor has chosen a branch, make it obvious which one they are looking at. Show the branch name clearly near the top of the page and use that location's phone number in calls to action, rather than a single head-office number that forces a transfer. On mobile, phone numbers should be tappable so calling takes one touch.

Customers should never have to wonder which location they are looking at, or which one they are calling.

Your website is only part of the picture. Each branch should also have its own business profile on Google and the major directories, with the same name, address, phone number and hours that appear on its location page. Link each profile to its own branch page instead of your homepage, so people who find you on a map land exactly where they need to be. Help search engines and AI tools understand each branch

People are not the only ones reading your location pages. Search engines and AI assistants also try to work out where your branches are, what each one offers and when it is open. The clearer and more consistent your information, the more confidently they can point nearby customers to the right place.

Structured data is a useful tool here. Location markup is a small block of code, invisible to visitors, that labels each page's business name, address, phone number, opening hours and map coordinates in a format machines can read without guessing. Each branch page should carry its own markup describing that specific location, while your main site describes the organization as a whole. Our article on structuring your data for AI-powered search explains where to start.

Internal links help, too. Your locations hub should link to every branch, each branch page should link to the services offered there, and your service pages should point visitors toward the locations where those services are available. This web of links helps visitors explore and gives search engines a clear picture of how your business fits together.

Above all, keep details consistent everywhere they appear. If your website, your business profiles and a directory listing each show slightly different hours for the same branch, both people and machines are left to guess which one is correct. Small mismatches like these are entirely avoidable with a single source of truth for each location's details.

Build a template so growth becomes routine

The real test of a multi-location website comes when you open your next branch. If launching its page means calling a developer, redesigning the locations section and writing everything from scratch, the structure has not scaled. If it means filling in a form, it has.

The answer is a location template. Instead of designing each branch page individually, your site uses one layout with clearly defined fields: name, address, hours, phone, team members, services offered, photos and a short introduction. Adding a new location becomes a matter of entering the details, uploading a few photos and pressing publish. The design, structured data and links to shared content all fall into place automatically.

Pair the template with a short launch checklist: create the page, set up the business profile, add the branch to the finder and footer, and confirm the new phone number and hours match everywhere.

Agree on who keeps each page current

Growth also brings more people who need to update information. Decide early who owns what. Branch managers are often best placed to know about changed hours or new staff, while a central team keeps the brand voice and shared content consistent. A simple process where managers submit changes and one person publishes them keeps things accurate without creating a bottleneck.

Plan for the less exciting changes as well. When a branch moves or closes, redirect its old page to the new address or the nearest remaining location instead of simply deleting it, so customers and search engines are guided somewhere useful. Ongoing website maintenance makes these updates quick and keeps the whole site healthy as it grows.

Ready to build a website that grows with your business?

Whether you are opening your second branch or your twentieth, the right structure makes every new location easier than the last. A clear hierarchy, genuine location pages, shared content that stays consistent and a reusable template turn your website from a source of confusion into something that supports your expansion.
